# The 12 Habits With the Highest Return on Life (English Version)

Let's run through the 12 habits with the highest return on your entire life.

You get up early, you check the boxes, you read, you hit the gym — you feel like you're working hard. But five years go by, your income hasn't moved, your relationships haven't changed, and your anxiety has only gotten deeper. The problem isn't that you're not working hard enough. The problem is that you picked the wrong habits. Some habits, kept up for a year, pay off in a straight line. Some habits, kept up for three years, pay off exponentially. The 12 I'm about to give you belong to the second kind. Burn these 12 high-leverage moves into your brain.

**One: Write a 100-word debrief every day.** Listen carefully — not a diary, not a play-by-play of your day. Write specifically: what decision did I make today, how did it turn out, and how do I fix it next time. Research out of Harvard Business School shows that people with a reflection habit learn 23% more efficiently than people without one. Your brain won't distill experience on its own — you have to force it. By turning the day's choices into data on paper, you claw back control from the invisible algorithms running your life.

**Two: Reach out to one person you've fallen out of touch with, every week.** Not a mass-blast holiday greeting — a genuine "how have you been?" The sociologist Mark Granovetter proved it back in 1973 with his theory of "the strength of weak ties": the opportunities that actually change your fate tend to come from the people you *don't* talk to often, because your close circle is stuck in roughly the same situation you are.

**Three: Carve out 30 minutes of deep thinking every day.** Put your phone in another room. No music, no scrolling. Just sit there and ask: what is the single most important, most painful problem in front of me right now? It sounds simple, but of everyone I know, fewer than 5% can keep this up for more than a month. There's a brutal rule in behavioral psychology: most people would rather doomscroll for two hours than face 30 minutes alone with themselves. You have to cut off the cheap dopamine of instant feedback and leave your brain some high-value strategic white space.

**Four: Build your own channel for putting knowledge out.** It can be X, it can be Instagram, it can be a YouTube channel. Remember — this move is not about pleasing followers or chasing a bigger audience. It's about forcing yourself to actually take an idea apart. The core logic of the Feynman technique is a test: can you explain a complex concept in plain language a five-year-old could follow? If you can't, then at the deepest level you never understood it — you were just bookmarking things to numb yourself.

**Five: Force yourself to read one serious book outside your field every month.** If you're in tech, go read marketing. If you're in sales, go dig into behavioral economics. Cross-domain knowledge is the hardest competitive moat you can build on a complex playing field. I have a friend in traditional sales who took this advice and grinded through Daniel Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow*. He took the "anchoring effect" from that book and quickly applied it to his project pricing strategy — and that year, with the whole industry in a slump, his personal numbers shot up 40% against the trend. Your competitors are all reading the same industry fast food. Read something different, and your commercial instincts pull a whole generation ahead of theirs.

**Six: Do one thing that makes you slightly uncomfortable every day.** Not self-harm, not torturing yourself — deliberately rubbing up against the edge of your comfort zone. It can be being the first to raise your hand in a meeting. It can be firmly turning down an unreasonable request. It can be paying a passing stranger a sincere compliment. In psychology this is called "graded exposure." Every time you push through a little fear, your brain recalibrates its defense mechanism against the unknown.

**Seven: Manage what information you take in.** You have to understand this: every piece of garbage information you consume today is deciding — pixel by pixel — the boundary of what you'll be able to think tomorrow. When I started making content online three years ago, the biggest change wasn't the bit of pocket change I earned — it was that I began an aggressive cleanse of my information sources. I muted and unfollowed every account that only manufactured anxiety with no real, actionable substance. Information carries a hidden toxicity; the residue of junk information contaminates your judgment like a virus.

**Eight: Do a financial asset review once a week.** Not penny-pinching over every few dollars in a spending log — but looking at where the real money went this week: which of it was an investment in assets that compound over the long term, and which was pure consumption burned away. Buffett said it: if you can't manage small money, you'll never manage big money. The vast majority of ordinary people are permanently fuzzy about their own net worth — and in any competitive game, fuzzy means completely out of control.

**Nine: Build a pre-sleep offline ritual.** Go offline for the hour before you close your eyes. Data from the Stanford sleep research center shows that heavy phone use before bed cuts your deep sleep that night by more than 20%. You think lying there staring at a screen is relaxing — but your cortex is still being dragged along at high speed by the algorithm. It's slow-motion self-destruction.

**Ten: Take at least one solo trip every year.** Not a photo-op at some viral hotspot to post on Instagram — going alone into a completely unfamiliar field of energy and forcing yourself to reckon with who you really are. At 25 I hit a serious survival crisis. I went alone into the mountains of Montana and stayed for ten full days. No itinerary, no social apps. And in that stretch of night with no echo coming back, I finally thought through the strategic direction for the rest of my life — I saw clearly what I was actually afraid of, and what I was running from.

**Eleven: Build a sense of boundaries. Have the courage to say no.** This is the most counter-instinctive and the hardest of the 12 to actually do. Psychology says that every time you say "sure" to fit in and please someone, you're spending your severely limited asset — your attention — to help someone else reach *their* goals for free. The bedrock of energy management was never about how to do more things; it's about how to refuse the unnecessary ones. Every piece of nonsense you agree to is a death sentence for your most important future opportunity.

**Twelve: Tell yourself one cold, hard truth every day.** Not reciting cheap motivational slogans — genuinely, even cruelly, examining yourself late at night: was I actually avoiding something today? The suffering of most honest, decent people doesn't come from being crushed by the outside world at all — it comes from that voice deep inside, the real one that's been suppressed and lied to for so long. The sooner you dare to settle accounts with your own weakness, the sooner you can make the clear-eyed choices that actually belong to a winner.

---

None of these 12 habits is new. You've probably heard them all. But "having heard it" and "having done it" are two completely different worlds. Open the Notes app on your phone right now, copy these 12 habits in, and then pick just one — just one — and start it today. Don't be greedy. Greed equals giving up. Pick the one that makes you the most uncomfortable — because that's the one with the highest return.
